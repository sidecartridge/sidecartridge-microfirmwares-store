Step 10 · Atari ST

Publish to the store.

Your microfirmware works on your board. This last step makes it something other people's boards can find, which closes the loop back to the catalogue you are reading this on.

Give it a real UUID

Everything so far used the shared development UUID. That has to change before anything leaves your desk. Two published apps with the same UUID fight over the same settings sector, and the catalogue cannot tell them apart.

Generate a UUIDv4 and keep it. It identifies this app for the rest of its life.

macOS / Linux

Terminal 
uuidgen
# or, if you would rather not care which tools are installed:
python3 -c "import uuid; print(uuid.uuid4())"

Windows (PowerShell)

PowerShell 
[guid]::NewGuid().ToString()
Write it down somewhere permanent.

Every future release of this app must use the same UUID. That is how an update is recognised as an update rather than a different app. Losing it means your users get a stranger instead of a new version. Put it in your README.

Make a release build

Terminal 
./build.sh pico_w release <YOUR UUID4 HERE>

Note both changes from step 6: release instead of debug, and your own UUID instead of the development one. You get the same three files in dist/, now named after your UUID:

  • <uuid>.uf2, the firmware people will download and install.
  • <uuid>.json, the metadata describing your app.
  • <uuid>.md5, the checksum that lets a download be verified.

Flash this build and test it once more. A release build is not identical to a debug build, and "it worked in debug" has surprised people before.

Host it, then get listed

Publishing is two separate things, and keeping them apart in your head helps:

  1. You host your own files. The .uf2 and an apps.json describing it live somewhere you control: a GitHub release, a web host, anywhere reachable. You keep ownership of your own listing.
  2. This store points at them. You open a pull request adding your origin to the registry. A build job then folds your listing into the catalogue every device downloads.

The full contract for apps.json, every field, the rules, a worked example and the exact pull request to open, is documented separately and kept up to date with the build that validates it:

Read the creator guide

What to expect from the pull request.

An automated check validates your JSON strictly and reports on the PR. A maintainer reviews it. Once merged, a scheduled job fetches your apps.json, folds it into the consolidated catalogue and deploys, so your app appears without anyone editing the catalogue by hand.

A couple of things the build does to your data are worth knowing before they surprise you. Device names are normalised to a standard spelling, and descriptions are sanitised. The creator guide explains both.

The two sets of channel names

This catches people out. Both this store and Booster let you choose a pre-release channel, and they do not use the same words for them.

This store calls it Booster's picker calls it Who it is for
stable, the default Stable Everyone. What a visitor sees with no parameters.
test, via ?channel=test Beta Testers, ahead of a stable release.
dev, via ?channel=dev Development You, while it is still moving.

Same three tiers, different labels. If you publish to the dev channel here and then go looking for "dev" in Booster, you want Development.

When in doubt, use a custom URL.

Booster also accepts a catalogue URL directly. Pointing it at the exact file you published removes the guesswork entirely, and it is the fastest way to test your own listing before it is public.

The loop closes

By default, Booster fetches its catalogue from http://atarist.sidecartridge.com/apps.json, and that is precisely the origin this store consolidates into the listing you can browse right now.

So the thing you publish is the thing the device downloads. Somebody with a SidecarTridge, an Atari ST and no idea who you are opens Booster, sees your app in the list, installs it.

You have finished the track.

You installed a toolchain, flashed a bootloader, built from a template, learned the memory map, wrote something of your own, debugged it on real 1980s hardware and published it.
